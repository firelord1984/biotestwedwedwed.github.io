DNA Structure

What is DNA? Know cold
Full name: Deoxyribonucleic Acid — a large, fragile molecule that always stays inside the nucleus for protection
3 functions: Heredity (pass info to offspring), Replication (copy itself for cell division), Protein Synthesis (code to build proteins = traits)
Shape: Double Helix — two strands twisted into a spiral, connected by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases
Nucleotides — the monomer of DNA
A nucleotide = phosphate group + deoxyribose sugar + nitrogenous base. Nucleotides link together to form the DNA strand.
4 Bases

A — Adenine
T — Thymine
C — Cytosine
G — Guanine

Chargaff's Rule

A pairs with T (and vice versa)
G pairs with C (and vice versa)
So %A always equals %T, and %G always equals %C

Example: If a DNA strand is 15% C → 15% G → 30% total. Remaining 70% is A+T → 35% each

DNA Replication

Replication is Semiconservative S phase, in the nucleus
Semiconservative means each new DNA molecule keeps one original strand + one newly built strand.
  • 1
    Helicase breaks hydrogen bonds between base pairs → unzips the helix → forms a Y-shaped replication fork
  • 2
    DNA Polymerase reads each template strand, attaches complementary nucleotides (A→T, G→C), and proofreads to catch errors
  • 3
    Result: 2 identical DNA molecules, each with one old strand and one new strand

Protein Synthesis — The Central Dogma

Overview
DNA → (Transcription) → mRNA → (Translation) → Protein
DNA can't leave the nucleus, so mRNA acts as a messenger to carry the gene's instructions to the ribosome.
RNA typeFull nameJob
mRNAMessenger RNADisposable copy of one gene; carries codons (3-base sequences) to ribosome
rRNARibosomal RNAForms the ribosome structure; forms peptide bonds between amino acids
tRNATransfer RNA"Decoder" — has anticodon (complementary to mRNA codon); carries its specific amino acid
RNA differences from DNA: Single-stranded · Uses ribose sugar · Uses Uracil (U) instead of Thymine · Can leave the nucleus
Transcription In the nucleus
  • 1
    RNA Polymerase binds to the gene and unzips that section of DNA
  • 2
    RNA Polymerase reads one DNA strand (template strand) and builds a complementary mRNA strand. Pairing: A→U, T→A, G→C, C→G
  • 3
    mRNA detaches, DNA rezips. mRNA exits the nucleus and travels to a ribosome.
Translation At the ribosome (cytoplasm)
  • 1
    Initiation: Ribosome attaches to mRNA and starts at the start codon AUG. First tRNA brings its amino acid.
  • 2
    Elongation: Ribosome shifts codon by codon. Each tRNA's anticodon matches the mRNA codon. Ribosome forms a peptide bond linking amino acids.
  • 3
    Termination: Ribosome reaches a stop codon → everything detaches → polypeptide is released and becomes a protein.
Working a Protein Synthesis Problem
Step 1: Transcribe DNA to mRNA — write complementary bases, replacing T with U
Step 2: Underline/bracket every 3 bases on mRNA (those are your codons)
Step 3: Use the codon-amino acid table to look up the amino acid for each codon
Step 4: List the amino acids in order → that's your polypeptide

Mitosis vs. Meiosis

Key differences at a glance
MitosisMeiosis
Cell typeSomatic (body) cellsGerm cells (in ovaries/testes)
PurposeGrowth, repair, asexual reproductionProduce gametes (sperm & egg)
Result2 diploid daughter cells (2n = 46)4 haploid daughter cells (n = 23)
Genetic diversityClones — genetically identicalAll unique — crossing over + independent assortment
DivisionsOne divisionTwo divisions (Meiosis I and II)
Diploid vs. Haploid
Diploid (2n = 46)

Two full sets of chromosomes. All somatic cells and germ cells before meiosis are diploid.

Haploid (n = 23)

One set of chromosomes. Only gametes (sperm & egg). Gametes need half so fertilization restores 46.

The Phases of Meiosis

Meiosis I — Separating Homologous Chromosomes
  • P I
    Prophase I — Homologs pair up into tetrads. Crossing over occurs at points called chiasmata. This is the KEY event that creates genetic diversity!
  • M I
    Metaphase I — Tetrads line up randomly at the middle (independent assortment) — orientation is 50/50 for each pair
  • A I
    Anaphase I — Homologous chromosomes separate to opposite poles (sister chromatids stay together)
  • T I
    Telophase I — 2 haploid cells form, each with one chromosome from each homologous pair (but still as double-stranded chromatids)
Meiosis II — Separating Sister Chromatids Like mitosis!
  • P II
    Prophase II — Chromosomes condense again in both cells
  • M II
    Metaphase II — Individual chromosomes line up at the middle
  • A II
    Anaphase II — Sister chromatids separate to opposite poles
  • T II
    Telophase II — 4 haploid (n = 23) unique daughter cells. These become gametes.

3 Sources of Genetic Variation

Why every offspring is unique
1. Crossing Over (Prophase I) — Non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes swap segments. Every chromatid ends up with a unique mix of maternal and paternal DNA.
2. Independent Assortment (Metaphase I) — Each pair of homologs lines up randomly. With 23 pairs, that's 2²³ ≈ 8.4 million possible chromosome combinations per gamete.
3. Random Fertilization — Any one of 8.4 million unique sperm can fertilize any one of 8.4 million unique eggs → essentially infinite genetic diversity.
Homologous chromosomes vs. sister chromatids
Homologous Chromosomes

Matching pairs — one from mom, one from dad. Same size, shape, centromere location, and gene locations (but may have different alleles). Separate in Meiosis I.

Sister Chromatids

Identical copies of ONE chromosome, connected at the centromere after DNA replication. Separate in Meiosis II (and Mitosis).

Chromosomal Mutations

Nondisjunction
Nondisjunction = chromosomes or chromatids fail to separate during Meiosis I or II. The resulting gametes have too many or too few chromosomes.
Trisomy (2n+1): 3 copies of one chromosome. Example: Down Syndrome = Trisomy 21
Monosomy (2n−1): Only 1 copy of a chromosome. Example: Turner Syndrome = single X (XO)
Klinefelter Syndrome: XXY — extra X in males
Survival note: Depends on which chromosome is affected. Having one X is not fatal, but having only a Y chromosome is — too many essential genes are on X.
Chromosomal Aberrations from Crossing-Over Errors
Deletion — segment lost Duplication — segment copied twice Inversion — segment flipped Translocation — segment moved to non-homologous chromosome
Cri du Chat Syndrome: Deletion from chromosome 5
Emanuel Syndrome: Translocation of chromosomes 11 and 22

Key Vocabulary

Essential terms
Gene: A segment of DNA that codes for a specific protein → determines a trait
Allele: A variant of a gene (e.g., T or t for height)
Dominant: The allele expressed when at least one copy is present (uppercase letter)
Recessive: Only expressed when both alleles are recessive (lowercase letter)
Homozygous: Both alleles are the same (TT or tt)
Heterozygous: Two different alleles (Tt)
Genotype: The actual allele combination an organism has (Tt)
Phenotype: The expressed physical trait (e.g., Tall)
Mendel's Laws
Law of Segregation: Allele pairs separate during Anaphase I of meiosis → each gamete receives exactly ONE allele per gene
P generation: True-breeding (homozygous) parents crossed for opposite traits
F1 generation: All hybrids (heterozygous) — show dominant trait
F2 generation: ~3:1 ratio of dominant to recessive phenotypes
Test cross: Cross an unknown dominant with a homozygous recessive. Any recessive offspring → the unknown parent was heterozygous (Bb, not BB)

Complete Dominance (Mendelian)
Rule: The dominant allele fully masks the recessive allele. Heterozygous (Tt) looks identical to homozygous dominant (TT).
Phenotypes: Only 2 — dominant OR recessive. No in-between.
Common mistake: Dominant does NOT mean "more common in the population" — it just means expressed when one copy is present
Incomplete Dominance
Rule: Neither allele is fully dominant → heterozygous has an intermediate (blended) phenotype
Notation: Use two different uppercase letters (RR, WW, RW) since neither is dominant
Example problem: Pink (RW) × Red (RR) → 50% Red (RR), 50% Pink (RW) → no white offspring possible
To get maximum pink: Cross Red × White → 100% Pink offspring
Codominance
Rule: Both alleles are equally dominant → heterozygous expresses BOTH phenotypes simultaneously (not blended)
Key difference from incomplete: Spots/stripes (both visible separately), not a blend
Example: Spotted cow (BW) × Spotted cow (BW) → 25% Black (BB), 50% Spotted (BW), 25% White (WW)

Polygenic Traits

Many genes → continuous variation
Definition: The phenotype is controlled by multiple genes on different chromosomes, each contributing to the trait
How to identify: Phenotypes appear as a continuous gradient (bell curve) in a population, not distinct categories
Examples: Human skin color (controlled by 3+ genes), height, hair color
Why couples can have very different-looking children: Each child inherits a different random combination of dominant and recessive alleles across multiple genes

Sex-Linked Traits

Genes on the X chromosome
Key idea: Males (XY) only have ONE X chromosome. A single recessive allele on their X will be expressed because there is no second X to mask it. Females (XX) can be carriers.
Carrier female: X^N X^c — has one normal and one recessive allele. Doesn't express the trait but can pass it to children.
Affected male: X^c Y — only one X, so the recessive allele is automatically expressed
Notation: Write alleles as superscripts on the X. Example: X^N = normal vision, X^c = color blind allele
Examples of sex-linked traits: Color blindness, hemophilia, ALD (adrenoleukodystrophy)

Pedigrees
Squares = males · Circles = females · Filled shape = affected individual

Spotting a recessive trait: Two unaffected parents have an affected child → both parents must be carriers (Aa × Aa)
Spotting a dominant trait: Trait appears in every generation (no skipping)
